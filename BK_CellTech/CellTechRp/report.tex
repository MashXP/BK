    \begin{abstract}
    \vspace{-1.5em}
    This study presents a standardized laboratory workflow for the isolation and establishment of a primary cell suspension from shrimp hepatopancreatic tissue. The procedure integrated mechanical mincing with enzymatic dissociation using 0.25\% Trypsin in Leibovitz L-15 medium, followed by sequential filtration through 100 $\mu$m and 40 $\mu$m mesh to ensure optimal single-cell recovery. The resulting suspension was concentrated via centrifugation at 500g for 10 minutes. Cell viability and density were subsequently quantified using the Trypan blue exclusion assay in a 1:1 dilution ratio via hemocytometer analysis. The protocol successfully yielded a viable primary cell suspension, demonstrating that the synergy of mechanical and enzymatic treatments is an effective approach for harvesting crustacean cells. These findings provide a foundational methodology for downstream applications in marine biotechnology, pathobiology, and in vitro cellular assays.
    \end{abstract}
    \vspace{0.5em}
    \noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Shrimp; Primary cell culture; Hepatopancreas; Enzymatic dissociation; Trypan blue exclusion; Cell viability.

To equip the students with knowledge and techniques in microbiology research, all the beginning to the end of the bacterial culture manually have been guided with some typical techniques like media preparation, bacterial culture, colony isolation, biochemical test, gram staining, and of most the bacteria density quantification.

% The chosen medium for bacterial cultivation was Luria-Bertani (LB) medium. This is the most commonly used in microbiology. Variations of the LB recipe can be found in the literature and lab manuals. The broth medium and slant agar medium were used for bacteria isolation.

% In biochemical testing, The Kligler Iron Agar (KIA) medium is a differential medium developed by I. J. Kligler in 1918 [1], combining his work on a hydrogen sulfide (H2S) detection medium with Russell's double sugar agar. Classification bacteria based on their ability to ferment glucose and lactose, or use peptone for main nutrions, produce gas, and generate hydrogen sulfide (H2S). The medium contains two sugar sources (glucose and lactose), peptone, iron, and phenol red as a pH indicator. Phenol red turns yellow in acidic conditions (pH $<$ 6.8) and red in alkaline conditions (pH $>$ 7.4). A yellow color indicates acid production from carbohydrate fermentation. If only glucose is fermented, the bottom (butt) remains yellow (acidic) while the slant reverts to red (alkaline) as bacteria oxidatively deaminate peptone under aerobic conditions. A yellow slant and yellow butt indicate fermentation of both glucose and lactose, maintaining an acidic environment throughout the medium. The formation of bubbles or cracks in the agar signifies gas released during carbohydrate fermentation, like Lactate dehydrogenation. A black precipitate forms when hydrogen sulfide reacts with ferrous sulfate in the medium, indicating H\textsubscript{2}S production.

% Some basic physical characteristics of bacteria were examined, including motility and Gram staining. The motility test is used to determine whether the bacteria possess structures such as flagella or cilia, allowing them to move actively in semi-liquid environments. The Gram staining test differentiates bacteria based on the structural composition of their cell walls, the peptidoglycan [2]. Gram-positive bacteria possess a thick peptidoglycan layer that retains the crystal violet iodine complex, appearing purple or blue under the microscope. Gram-negative bacteria have a thinner peptidoglycan layer located between two lipid cell membranes. During the alcohol decolorization step, the crystal violet is removed, and subsequent staining with safranin dyes the inner membrane, resulting in pink to red-colored cells under the microscope.

\section{Results \& Discussion}
The cell isolation from shrimp hepatopancreas was quantified using a hemocytometer after a 1:1 dilution (10 $\mu$L cell suspension + 10 $\mu$L Trypan Blue). The resulting suspension was resuspended in 1 mL.

\subsection{Calculations}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Percentage Viability:}
    \begin{equation*}\small
    \%V = \frac{\sum L}{\sum T} \times 100 = \frac{299}{354} \times 100 \approx 84.5\%
    \end{equation*}

    \item \textbf{Live Cell Concentration:} \\
    (DF = 2 due to 1:1 mix)
    \begin{equation*}\small
    \text{Cells/mL} = \frac{\sum L}{5} \times DF \times 10^4 = 1.196 \times 10^6
    \end{equation*}

    \item \textbf{Total Yield (1 mL):}
    \begin{equation*}\small
    \text{Yield} = 1.196 \times 10^6 \text{ viable cells}
    \end{equation*}
\end{enumerate}

The data demonstrates a successful isolation with a viability of 84.5\%, exceeding the 80\% benchmark for healthy primary cultures. The 0.25\% Trypsin treatment (10 min) effectively dissociated the tissue without significant membrane damage. The concentration ($\approx 1.2 \times 10^6$ cells/mL) indicates a high recovery rate. Minor localized viability decreases (Top/Left) are negligible compared to the robust overall population.

\section{Conclusion}
The protocol involving trypsinization, sequential filtration (100$\rightarrow$40 $\mu$m), and resuspension yielded a high-density, viable cell suspension ($1.196 \times 10^6$ cells, 84.5\% viability), optimized for downstream applications.
