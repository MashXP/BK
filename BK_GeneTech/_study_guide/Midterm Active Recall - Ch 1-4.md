## Chapter 1 — Nucleic Acids Extraction & Purification

> [!question]- **Q1.** Name the four sequential stages of nucleic acid extraction.
> **A:** Cell lysis → phase separation → washing → elution/precipitation.

> [!question]- **Q2.** Name the four barriers you must breach, per cell type.
> **A:** Animal = phospholipid bilayer only. Plant = cellulose/pectin cell wall. Bacteria = peptidoglycan wall (Gram⁺ thick; Gram⁻ outer membrane + thin peptidoglycan). Eukaryotes also need nuclear membrane lysis.

> [!question]- **Q3.** Why do DNA, RNA and plasmid extraction share the same core principles?
> **A:** All are polyanionic and hydrophilic — the negatively charged phosphate backbone dominates their chemistry.

> [!question]- **Q4.** List the three extraction method classes.
> **A:** Liquid-phase (phenol-chloroform, salting-out/Proteinase K), solid-phase (silica spin columns), magnetic bead.

> [!question]- **Q5.** Function of SDS in a lysis buffer.
> **A:** Anionic detergent — solubilises membrane lipids into mixed micelles and denatures proteins.

> [!question]- **Q6.** Function of Tris-HCl.
> **A:** pH buffering, typically pH 8.0, which keeps DNA in the aqueous phase.

> [!question]- **Q7.** Function of EDTA — and which ions it targets.
> **A:** Chelates Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺, stripping nucleases (DNases/RNases) of their catalytic cofactors → no degradation.

> [!question]- **Q8.** What does phenol-chloroform do to proteins?
> **A:** Disrupts secondary/tertiary structure, exposes hydrophobic residues, making proteins insoluble in the aqueous phase.

> [!question]- **Q9.** Label the three layers of a phenol-chloroform tube and what each contains.
> **A:** Top = aqueous (DNA/RNA + salts). Middle = interphase (denatured proteins, debris). Bottom = organic (phenol, chloroform, lipids, hydrophobic compounds).

> [!question]- **Q10.** Which layer do you collect, and why repeat extraction 1–3×?
> **A:** Upper aqueous phase, pipetted without disturbing the interphase. Repeat until the interphase is completely clear.

> [!question]- **Q11.** Why add monovalent cations (Na⁺/NH₄⁺) before alcohol precipitation?
> **A:** They neutralise the repulsive negative charges of the phosphate backbones.

> [!question]- **Q12.** How does ethanol/isopropanol precipitate DNA?
> **A:** It lowers the dielectric constant of the solvent, driving nucleic acids out of solution.

> [!question]- **Q13.** Why wash the pellet with 70% ethanol?
> **A:** It dissolves co-precipitated salts without dissolving the long nucleic acid chains.

> [!question]- **Q14.** In what do you resuspend the final pellet, and how do you store it?
> **A:** Nuclease-free water or TE (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). Short term 4 °C; long term −20 to −30 °C, −80 °C for RNA.

> [!question]- **Q15.** What is Proteinase K, and what makes it useful?
> **A:** A serine protease cleaving peptide bonds adjacent to aliphatic/aromatic amino acids; stays active with SDS, EDTA and at 50–65 °C. It inactivates nucleases and frees DNA from histones.

> [!question]- **Q16.** Proteinase K vs phenol-chloroform: purity and hazard.
> **A:** ProtK = very high purity, safe, simple, but expensive. Phenol = strong removal, cheap, but toxic/volatile and residual solvent can inhibit PCR.

> [!question]- **Q17.** State the two conditions required for DNA to bind silica.
> **A:** High chaotropic salt concentration + acidic/neutral pH (≤ 7.0).

> [!question]- **Q18.** Draw/describe the cation bridge.
> **A:** Silica-O⁻ ⋯ Na⁺ ⋯ ⁻O-P-DNA.

> [!question]- **Q19.** How do chaotropic salts enable binding?
> **A:** They disrupt the ordered hydrogen-bond network of water, dehydrating both the silica surface and the phosphate backbone.

> [!question]- **Q20.** State the two conditions for elution.
> **A:** Low salt (10 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.5, TE, or nuclease-free water pH ≥ 7.0) + alkaline pH. Low ionic strength restores the hydration shell and breaks the cation bridge.

> [!question]- **Q21.** What do the wash buffers contain, and what do they remove?
> **A:** 70–80% ethanol — washes out unbound proteins, polysaccharides, lipids and residual chaotropic salts while DNA stays immobilised.

> [!question]- **Q22.** What is the magnetic bead core, and why choose this method?
> **A:** Superparamagnetic Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles with silica/functional coatings. No centrifugation, automatable on 96/384-well robots, high purity, intact (shear-free) nucleic acids.

> [!question]- **Q23.** Rank the three methods by DNA yield.
> **A:** Phenol-chloroform 900–1000 ng > Proteinase K 700–800 ng > silica column 100–400 ng.

> [!question]- **Q24.** Rank them by time and by required sample input.
> **A:** Time: column 30–45 min < ProtK 2–2.5 h < phenol 2–4 h. Input: column 100–200 µL (low); phenol/ProtK 500 µL–1.5 mL.

> [!question]- **Q25.** State the Beer-Lambert law and each term.
> **A:** A = ε·c·l. A = absorbance, c = concentration, ε = extinction coefficient, l = pathlength (1.0 cm).

> [!question]- **Q26.** Give ε for dsDNA, RNA, ssDNA.
> **A:** dsDNA 0.020, RNA 0.025, ssDNA ≈ 0.030 mL/(µg·cm).

> [!question]- **Q27.** Concentration formulas for dsDNA, RNA, ssDNA at 1 cm pathlength.
> **A:** dsDNA = A₂₆₀ × 50 µg/mL; RNA = A₂₆₀ × 40 µg/mL; ssDNA = A₂₆₀ × 33 µg/mL.

> [!question]- **Q28.** What absorbs at 260, 280, and 230 nm?
> **A:** 260 = purine/pyrimidine aromatic rings (nucleic acids). 280 = aromatic amino acids Trp/Tyr/Phe (protein). 230 = chaotropic salts, EDTA, phenol, carbohydrates, peptides.

> [!question]- **Q29.** Give the purity thresholds for DNA and RNA.
> **A:** DNA 260/280 ≈ 1.80 (1.70–1.90), 260/230 1.80–2.00. RNA 260/280 ≈ 2.00 (1.90–2.10), 260/230 2.00–2.20.

> [!question]- **Q30.** Why is RNA's 260/280 higher than DNA's?
> **A:** Uracil absorbs more strongly at 260 nm than thymine.

> [!question]- **Q31.** A 260/280 of 1.5 and a 260/230 of 1.2 — name the likely contaminants.
> **A:** 260/280 < 1.6 → protein/phenol carryover. 260/230 < 1.8 → guanidinium/chaotropic salts, EDTA, polysaccharides.

> [!question]- **Q32.** Alkaline lysis: what is in each of Solutions I, II, III?
> **A:** I = glucose/Tris/EDTA + RNase A. II = NaOH + SDS. III = potassium acetate (pH 4.8–5.5).

> [!question]- **Q33.** Why does plasmid survive alkaline lysis while chromosomal DNA is removed?
> **A:** Covalently closed circular plasmids renature rapidly on neutralisation; large genomic DNA cannot renature and precipitates with the K-SDS/protein complex, pelleting on centrifugation. Plasmid stays in the supernatant.

## Chapter 2 — PCR & Electrophoresis

> [!question]- **Q34.** Name the five *in vivo* replication enzymes and their jobs.
> **A:** Helicase unwinds; SSB stabilises single strands; primase makes RNA primers (free 3'-OH); polymerase synthesises 5'→3' (Okazaki on lagging); ligase seals nicks between Okazaki fragments.

> [!question]- **Q35.** What does PCR substitute for that enzymatic machinery?
> **A:** Thermal cycling replaces helicase/SSB; synthetic primers replace primase.

> [!question]- **Q36.** Give the three PCR stages with temperatures and the physical event of each.
> **A:** Denaturation 92–96 °C (H-bonds break, strands separate); annealing 45–55 °C (primers hybridise); extension 72 °C (polymerase adds dNTPs 5'→3').

> [!question]- **Q37.** How do you set annealing temperature?
> **A:** ~T_m − 5 °C.

> [!question]- **Q38.** Amplification yield formula, and typical cycle number.
> **A:** N₀ × 2ⁿ; 25–35 cycles → up to 10⁹-fold.

> [!question]- **Q39.** The five components of a PCR mix.
> **A:** Thermostable DNA polymerase, template DNA, primers (F+R), dNTPs, reaction buffer (with Mg²⁺).

> [!question]- **Q40.** Why was Klenow (*E. coli* Pol I) impractical?
> **A:** Heat-labile — killed at 95 °C, so fresh enzyme had to be added after every cycle.

> [!question]- **Q41.** Taq: source organism, thermostability, optimum temperature.
> **A:** *Thermus aquaticus* (thermophile). Half-life > 40 min at 95 °C; optimum 72 °C.

> [!question]- **Q42.** Taq's two key weaknesses.
> **A:** No 3'→5' proofreading (error 10⁻⁴–10⁻⁵/bp); risk of non-specific amplification/gel smear.

> [!question]- **Q43.** What is the 3'-A overhang and why does it matter?
> **A:** Taq adds a non-template extra dA at 3' ends → enables direct TA cloning into T-vector.

> [!question]- **Q44.** High-fidelity polymerases: source, activity, error rate, product ends.
> **A:** Pfu from *Pyrococcus furiosus*; 3'→5' exonuclease proofreading; error 10⁻⁶–10⁻⁷ (50–100× better than Taq); blunt ends (incompatible with TA cloning).

> [!question]- **Q45.** Hot-start polymerase mechanism and activation.
> **A:** Catalytic centre reversibly blocked (antibody, chemical moiety, aptamer) below 60 °C → no non-specific priming or primer-dimer during room-temperature setup. Activated by initial heat pulse > 60–95 °C for 1–5 min.

> [!question]- **Q46.** Primer design: length, GC%, T_m, ΔT_m.
> **A:** 20–30 nt; 40–60% GC, uniform; T_m 55–65 °C; ΔT_m ≤ 2–5 °C; avoid >3 GC at the 3' end (GC clamp).

> [!question]- **Q47.** Wallace rule for T_m.
> **A:** T_m = 2 °C × (A+T) + 4 °C × (G+C).

> [!question]- **Q48.** Where does each primer bind, and which direction does it extend?
> **A:** Forward = identical to sense, binds antisense (bottom 3'→5') strand, extends rightward. Reverse = reverse complement of sense, binds sense (top 5'→3') strand, extends leftward.

> [!question]- **Q49.** Typical template amounts.
> **A:** 1–100 ng plasmid/viral DNA; 50–250 ng human genomic DNA per reaction.

> [!question]- **Q50.** dNTP concentration, and the effect of imbalance.
> **A:** 200 µM each of dATP/dTTP/dCTP/dGTP, equimolar. Imbalance increases misincorporation and error rate.

> [!question]- **Q51.** Mg²⁺ concentration, and the effect of too much / too little.
> **A:** 1.5–2.5 mM MgCl₂. Excess → non-specific bands; insufficient → no PCR product.

> [!question]- **Q52.** What does KCl do in the buffer?
> **A:** 50 mM, facilitates primer annealing.

> [!question]- **Q53.** First-generation thermocyclers: design and why mineral oil.
> **A:** Robotic arm moved tubes between three water baths (95 → 55 → 72 °C); mineral oil overlay prevented evaporation.

> [!question]- **Q54.** Modern thermocycler: heating element, ramp rate, lid.
> **A:** Peltier elements on aluminium/silver blocks, 3–6 °C/s ramp; heated lid at 105 °C prevents condensation, so no oil needed.

> [!question]- **Q55.** Monoplex vs multiplex PCR.
> **A:** Monoplex = one primer pair, one target. Multiplex = multiple primer pairs in one tube, multiple amplicons.

> [!question]- **Q56.** Multiplex design requirements and main limitation.
> **A:** All primer pairs must share matched T_m and produce amplicons separated by ≥ 30–50 bp. Limitation: primer competition/cross-hybridisation and dNTP-polymerase competition → unequal yields, primer-dimers.

> [!question]- **Q57.** Nested PCR architecture, and why it removes false positives.
> **A:** Round 1 outer primers flank the target; round 2 uses diluted round-1 product with inner primers binding inside the primary amplicon. Non-specific round-1 products lack inner primer sites, so they cannot amplify.

> [!question]- **Q58.** RT-PCR: the two steps, and the primers used for cDNA synthesis.
> **A:** Step 1 reverse transcriptase (RNA-dependent DNA polymerase) makes cDNA from RNA using oligo-dT, random hexamers, or gene-specific primers. Step 2 thermostable polymerase amplifies the cDNA.

> [!question]- **Q59.** What does qPCR eliminate from the workflow?
> **A:** Post-PCR agarose gel electrophoresis — fluorescence is read in real time each cycle.

> [!question]- **Q60.** SYBR Green: mechanism, advantage, limitation, required control.
> **A:** Intercalates the minor groove of any dsDNA, fluorescence ∝ dsDNA. Cheap, no probe design. Binds primer-dimers and off-target products → requires melt curve analysis (dF/dT) to confirm a single peak.

> [!question]- **Q61.** TaqMan probe: structure and signal generation.
> **A:** Sequence-specific oligo with 5' reporter fluorophore and 3' quencher; intact probe is quenched by FRET. During extension, Taq's 5'→3' exonuclease cleaves the probe, separating reporter from quencher → fluorescence.

> [!question]- **Q62.** TaqMan advantages over SYBR.
> **A:** Absolute sequence specificity; allows in-tube multiplexing with different dyes (FAM, VIC, CY5). Costs more, needs probe design.

> [!question]- **Q63.** Define C_t and its relation to starting copy number.
> **A:** The fractional cycle at which the amplification curve crosses the fluorescence threshold. C_t is inversely proportional to log₁₀ N₀: 10⁷ copies → C_t ≈ 15; 10¹ copies → C_t ≈ 35. NTC gives undetermined.

> [!question]- **Q64.** Efficiency formula, ideal slope, and R².
> **A:** E = 10^(−1/slope) − 1. Slope −3.32 = 100% efficiency, R² ≥ 0.99.

> [!question]- **Q65.** Why does DNA migrate toward the anode, and what is the mnemonic?
> **A:** The phosphate backbone gives a constant negative charge-to-mass ratio, so DNA runs cathode (−) → anode (+): "run to the red".

> [!question]- **Q66.** Relationship between mobility and fragment size.
> **A:** μ ∝ 1/log₁₀(bp). Small fragments move fast and far; large fragments stay near the wells.

> [!question]- **Q67.** Agarose percentages for large vs small fragments.
> **A:** 0.7–1.0% for 1–10 kb; 1.5–2.0% for 100–1000 bp.

> [!question]- **Q68.** TAE vs TBE.
> **A:** TAE = preparative gel extraction and large fragments. TBE = higher buffering capacity, sharper resolution for < 1 kb.

> [!question]- **Q69.** Two jobs of loading dye.
> **A:** Density agents (glycerol or Ficoll) sink the sample into the well; tracking dyes (bromophenol blue, xylene cyanol) show migration progress.

> [!question]- **Q70.** What does a DNA ladder give you?
> **A:** Known fragment sizes to determine amplicon size, plus approximate mass by comparative band brightness.

> [!question]- **Q71.** Staining dyes and detection wavelengths.
> **A:** Ethidium bromide, GelRed, SYBR Safe intercalate base pairs; UV 302–312 nm (or blue LED) → fluorescence.

## Chapter 3 — Vectors & Cloning

> [!question]- **Q72.** Define clone and cloning.
> **A:** Clone = any biological material (DNA, cell, organism) genetically identical to its source. Cloning = the technique for producing those identical copies.

> [!question]- **Q73.** The three levels of cloning.
> **A:** Molecular (DNA copies in a host), cellular (population from one progenitor cell), organismal (SCNT — Dolly; plant tissue culture).

> [!question]- **Q74.** The four components of a cloning system.
> **A:** Vector, enzymes (RE + ligase + modifiers), recombinant DNA molecule, host cell.

> [!question]- **Q75.** The four essential features of a cloning vector.
> **A:** Origin of replication (`ori`), selectable marker genes, multiple cloning site (MCS/polylinker), small physical size.

> [!question]- **Q76.** What does `ori` do?
> **A:** Recognised by host replication enzymes → autonomous replication without chromosomal integration; it sets plasmid copy number.

> [!question]- **Q77.** Why a selectable marker, with examples.
> **A:** Only cells that took up the vector survive antibiotic selection: Ampʳ/*bla*, Kanʳ, Cmʳ, Tetʳ.

> [!question]- **Q78.** Why small vector size (2–10 kb) helps.
> **A:** Easy manipulation, resistance to mechanical shear during isolation, higher transformation efficiency.

> [!question]- **Q79.** Cloning vector vs expression vector.
> **A:** Cloning = insert, maintain, amplify DNA (pUC19, pBR322). Expression = adds promoter, ribosome binding site/Shine-Dalgarno, terminator for transcription/translation (pET, pGEX).

> [!question]- **Q80.** pSC101 → pBR322 → pUC19: size, copy number, markers.
> **A:** pSC101 (1973, ~9.0 kb, 1–5 copies, Tetʳ, single *EcoRI* site). pBR322 (1977, 4361 bp, 15–20 copies, Tetʳ + Ampʳ, individual RE sites). pUC19 (1982, ~2.68 kb, 500+ copies, Ampʳ, clustered MCS).

> [!question]- **Q81.** Screening strategy for each generation.
> **A:** pSC101 = antibiotic only. pBR322 = insertional inactivation via replica plating. pUC = blue-white screening via *lacZ* α-complementation.

> [!question]- **Q82.** Explain blue-white selection.
> **A:** Intact *lacZα* produces the α-peptide, which complements the host's ΔM15 mutant ω-peptide → active β-galactosidase → cleaves X-Gal → blue. Insert in the MCS breaks the ORF → no α-peptide → white. No vector uptake → no Ampʳ → no growth. Plate = Amp + X-Gal + IPTG.

> [!question]- **Q83.** pET expression vector elements.
> **A:** T7 promoter (potent, needs T7 RNA polymerase from BL21(DE3)), *lacO* for IPTG-inducible control, *lacI* repressor (blocks leaky basal expression), 10× His-tag for Ni²⁺-IMAC, enterokinase site for tag removal, T7 terminator.

> [!question]- **Q84.** λ phage vector: genome, cos sites, dispensable region.
> **A:** 45–48.5 kbp linear dsDNA with cohesive cos ends; the central ~15–20 kbp lysogeny cassette is dispensable.

> [!question]- **Q85.** Why must insert be ligated between the λ arms, and what is the packaging window?
> **A:** Left + right arms alone (~30 kb) are below the ~38 kb minimum for capsid packaging. Recombinant concatemers in the 38–52 kbp window (78–105% of wild type) are packaged *in vitro*; insert capacity 9–23 kb.

> [!question]- **Q86.** λ advantages over plasmid transformation.
> **A:** Near-100% delivery efficiency by transduction vs CaCl₂ chemical transformation, larger insert capacity, rapid lytic replication (plaques/PFU).

> [!question]- **Q87.** The three cis-acting elements of a YAC and their jobs.
> **A:** 2 × TEL (telomeres — protect ends from exonucleases/end-replication loss), 1 × CEN (kinetochore, equal segregation), 1 × ARS (yeast origin of replication). Capacity 100 kb–1 Mb.

> [!question]- **Q88.** YAC selectable markers.
> **A:** *TRP1* (tryptophan) and *URA3* (uracil) auxotrophic markers, with internal *SUP4* carrying the cloning site; *BamHI* cleavage releases the spacer and exposes the telomeric ends.

> [!question]- **Q89.** What does a restriction endonuclease cleave, and what cofactor?
> **A:** Phosphodiester bonds on both strands of the duplex (plus H-bonds in staggered cuts); requires Mg²⁺.

> [!question]- **Q90.** The restriction-modification system.
> **A:** Unmethylated phage DNA is cut at palindromic sites and degraded. Host methylase (e.g. EcoRI methylase) transfers CH₃ from SAM to A(N6) or C(C5/N4) inside host recognition sites, sterically blocking the restriction enzyme.

> [!question]- **Q91.** Cut frequency for 4-, 6-, 8-bp cutters.
> **A:** (1/4)ⁿ: 4 bp = 1/256 bp (*HaeIII* GGCC); 6 bp = 1/4096 ≈ 4 kb (*EcoRV*, *PstI*); 8 bp = 1/65,536 ≈ 65.5 kb (*NotI* — rare cutter).

> [!question]- **Q92.** Sticky vs blunt ends, with examples.
> **A:** Sticky = staggered cut, single-stranded overhangs that H-bond with complements → high ligation efficiency (*HindIII* gives 5'-AGCT). Blunt = symmetric cut, no overhang, compatible with anything but ligates far less efficiently (*EcoRV*).

> [!question]- **Q93.** Restriction enzyme nomenclature.
> **A:** Genus (capital, italic) + species (lowercase, italic) + strain (non-italic) + Roman numeral for isolation order. BamHI = *Bacillus amyloliquefaciens* strain H, first. EcoRI = *E. coli* RY13. HindIII = *Haemophilus influenzae* Rd, third.

> [!question]- **Q94.** Type I vs II vs III restriction enzymes.
> **A:** Type I — cuts randomly >1000 bp from the site, needs ATP + SAM + Mg²⁺, unpredictable. Type II — cuts within/adjacent to a palindromic site, Mg²⁺ only, no ATP → the cloning workhorse. Type III — cuts 20–30 bp downstream, asymmetric, ATP + Mg²⁺.

> [!question]- **Q95.** Definition of one restriction enzyme unit.
> **A:** Amount that completely digests 1 µg of reference DNA (λ DNA) in 1 h at optimal temperature (usually 37 °C) in a standard buffer, typically 50 µL.

> [!question]- **Q96.** Why inactivate RE after digestion, and three ways to do it.
> **A:** Active enzyme keeps cutting, destroying sticky overhangs or re-cutting the newly circularised plasmid once ligase is added. Methods: heat 65–80 °C for 20 min (heat-labile enzymes), EDTA chelation of Mg²⁺ (10–20 mM), or column/phenol purification for thermostable enzymes.

> [!question]- **Q97.** Four criteria for choosing a restriction enzyme in cloning.
> **A:** Must be Type II; site must be in the MCS and unique (single-cutter) in the vector; recognition sequence must not occur inside the insert ORF/promoter; ideally heat-inactivatable.

> [!question]- **Q98.** *E. coli* DNA ligase vs T4 DNA ligase.
> **A:** *E. coli* — NAD⁺ cofactor, sticky ends only, inactive on blunt. T4 — ATP + Mg²⁺, sticky and blunt (plus RNA-DNA duplexes), the commercial workhorse; blunt ends need higher enzyme concentration and PEG.

> [!question]- **Q99.** Definition of one ligase unit.
> **A:** Amount that ligates 50% of 100 ng cohesive-end DNA in 50 µL with 1× ligase buffer, 30 min at 23 °C.

> [!question]- **Q100.** The five phases of the cloning pipeline.
> **A:** 1 Selection (host + vector) → 2 Preparation (purify vector and insert) → 3 Recombinant construct (digest + ligate) → 4 Host delivery (heat shock or electroporation) → 5 Screening/validation (antibiotic, X-Gal, colony PCR, sequencing).

> [!question]- **Q101.** Why treat the linearised vector with alkaline phosphatase (CIP/SAP)?
> **A:** 5'-dephosphorylation blocks vector self-ligation, suppressing empty-vector clones.

> [!question]- **Q102.** Ligation conditions and vector:insert ratio.
> **A:** T4 ligase in ATP buffer, 16 °C overnight or 22 °C for 1–2 h; molar ratio 1:3 or 1:5.

> [!question]- **Q103.** Chemical heat-shock transformation: reagent and pulse.
> **A:** Ice-cold CaCl₂ (shields DNA and membrane phospholipids), then 42 °C for 45–90 s to create transient bilayer pores, then back on ice.

> [!question]- **Q104.** Electroporation: conditions and efficiency.
> **A:** Electrocompetent cells in non-ionic glycerol/water, 1.8–2.5 kV pulse, 10⁹–10¹⁰ cfu/µg.

> [!question]- **Q105.** Why recover in SOC before plating?
> **A:** 45–60 min in rich antibiotic-free media at 37 °C lets the cells express the antibiotic resistance proteins before selection.

> [!question]- **Q106.** TA cloning principle.
> **A:** Taq's non-template terminal transferase adds a 3'-A to both ends of the amplicon; a linearised T-vector (pGEM-T, pCR2.1) has 3'-T overhangs; A:T pairing + T4 ligase, no restriction digest.

> [!question]- **Q107.** TA cloning: two advantages, two limitations.
> **A:** No RE digestion, no need to add restriction sites to primers, rapid. Limits: requires non-proofreading polymerase (Pfu/Q5 give blunt ends, need A-tailing); insertion is non-directional (50% forward, 50% reverse); T-vectors are expensive.

> [!question]- **Q108.** Insert capacity: pUC, pET, λ, YAC.
> **A:** pUC 0.1–10 kb; pET 0.5–5 kb; λ 9–23 kb; YAC 100–1000 kb.

## Chapter 4 — Blotting Methods

> [!question]- **Q109.** What is blotting, and why transfer off the gel?
> **A:** Detecting a specific target (DNA/RNA/protein) from a mixture after gel separation. Gels are fragile, diffuse, and impermeable to probes/antibodies, so molecules are immobilised on a durable membrane (nitrocellulose, nylon, PVDF).

> [!question]- **Q110.** Define nucleic acid hybridization, and the U/T rule.
> **A:** Spontaneous annealing of single strands into duplexes by Watson-Crick H-bonding. In RNA-DNA heteroduplexes, U in RNA pairs with A, functionally equivalent to T.

> [!question]- **Q111.** What is a probe, and what labels are used?
> **A:** A defined labelled ssDNA/RNA fragment (20–50 nt oligos up to cloned fragments of hundreds–thousands of nt), labelled with radioisotopes (³²P, ³⁵S, ³H), fluorophores, or haptens (DIG, biotin).

> [!question]- **Q112.** Southern blot: who, when, what it detects.
> **A:** Edwin Southern, 1975. Detection, sizing and quantification of target DNA in genomic DNA or recombinant constructs.

> [!question]- **Q113.** The six Southern blot steps.
> **A:** 1 DNA extraction + RE digestion → 2 agarose electrophoresis → 3 in-gel alkaline denaturation + capillary transfer → 4 fixation → 5 hybridization with labelled probe → 6 stringency wash + detection.

> [!question]- **Q114.** Southern denaturation buffer.
> **A:** 0.5 M NaOH + 1.5 M NaCl, then neutralise (Tris-HCl/NaCl).

> [!question]- **Q115.** Components of the capillary transfer stack, and what drives flow.
> **A:** Reservoir of 10–20× SSC, sponge wick, gel, bubble-free membrane on top, stack of paper towels, 0.5–1 kg weight. Upward capillary flow carries DNA out of the gel and traps it on the membrane.

> [!question]- **Q116.** Fixation for nitrocellulose vs nylon.
> **A:** Nitrocellulose: bake under vacuum 80 °C for 1–2 h. Nylon: shortwave UV at 254 nm.

> [!question]- **Q117.** What blocks non-specific binding before hybridization?
> **A:** Denhardt's solution, sheared salmon sperm DNA, SDS.

> [!question]- **Q118.** Define stringency, and the conditions for high vs low.
> **A:** How closely probe and target must match to stay paired. High stringency = low salt, high temperature, formamide → perfect matches only. Low stringency = high salt, lower temperature → allows partial matches / gene-family members.

> [!question]- **Q119.** Southern detection methods.
> **A:** Radioactive probe → autoradiography (X-ray film) or phosphorimager. Non-radioactive → chemiluminescent/chromogenic substrate, CCD imaging.

> [!question]- **Q120.** Four Southern applications.
> **A:** Gene mapping; structural variants (indels, duplications, translocations, rearrangements); RFLP (SNPs that create/abolish RE sites — diagnostics, carrier screening, forensics); transgene verification (integration, orientation, copy number).

> [!question]- **Q121.** Northern blot: who, when, purpose.
> **A:** Alwine, Kemp & Stark, 1977 (a pun on Southern). Detection, sizing and quantification of RNA, mainly mRNA, for expression levels and alternative splicing.

> [!question]- **Q122.** RNA integrity metric before loading.
> **A:** 28S:18S rRNA ratio ≥ 2.0.

> [!question]- **Q123.** Northern denaturing agents, and how formaldehyde works.
> **A:** Formaldehyde/formamide, or glyoxal/DMSO. Formaldehyde reacts with amino groups of the bases, disrupting H-bonding and linearising folded RNA.

> [!question]- **Q124.** Why NaOH denatures DNA but destroys RNA.
> **A:** DNA has no 2'-OH, so it is alkaline-stable. RNA's 2'-OH acts as a nucleophile attacking the adjacent 3',5'-phosphodiester bond under OH⁻, forming cyclic 2',3'-phosphate intermediates and hydrolysing RNA to mononucleotides.

> [!question]- **Q125.** Why positively charged nylon for RNA.
> **A:** Electrostatic affinity for negatively charged nucleic acids — binds short ssRNA far better than uncharged nitrocellulose; durable, not brittle after UV/baking, tolerates stripping and re-probing.

> [!question]- **Q126.** Northern applications.
> **A:** Tissue-specific and temporal expression profiling, responses to stimuli; RNA virus detection and viral load; transcript sizing for splice isoforms and polyadenylation variants.

> [!question]- **Q127.** Why RE digestion is mandatory for Southern but not Northern.
> **A:** Genomic DNA is millions–billions of bp and cannot enter/resolve a gel without RE cleavage into 0.5–20 kb fragments. RNA transcripts are already discrete, finite-length molecules.

> [!question]- **Q128.** Blotting vs PCR: input amount, prior sequence info, structural variants, turnaround, quantification.
> **A:** Blot: µg input, low/moderate sequence info needed (probe only), excellent for large uncharacterised variants, 2–4 days, semi-quantitative (densitometry). PCR: pg–ng, needs exact primer sites at both ends, poor for large variants, 2–4 h, non-quantitative (endpoint plateau). qPCR: pg–ng, primers + probe, 1–2 h, quantitative over >7 logs.

> [!question]- **Q129.** Define in situ hybridization and its advantages.
> **A:** Probe-target pairing directly inside fixed tissue sections, cell preparations, or intact colonies/plaques, with no prior nucleic acid purification. Preserves spatial/histological context; screens tens of thousands of clones at once.

> [!question]- **Q130.** Colony/plaque hybridization: who, and the workflow.
> **A:** Grunstein & Hogness 1975, Benton & Davis 1977. Master plate → membrane overlay 1–2 min (needle orientation marks) → alkaline lysis 0.5 M NaOH/1.5 M NaCl → neutralise, 2× SSC wash → UV/bake fixation → labelled probe → stringency wash → detect → align with master plate → pick viable positive clone.

> [!question]- **Q131.** Why needle marks on the master plate.
> **A:** Asymmetric orientation marks let you re-align the developed membrane with the plate and pick the exact viable clone, which stays unharmed on the master.

> [!question]- **Q132.** Western blot: target, matrix, transfer, membrane, detection.
> **A:** Protein; SDS-PAGE (or native PAGE); electroblotting; PVDF or nitrocellulose; primary + enzyme-conjugated secondary antibodies.

> [!question]- **Q133.** FISH: target and application.
> **A:** DNA/RNA in intact cells, metaphase spreads, tissue slices; no transfer; fluorophore-conjugated oligo probes; chromosome mapping, karyotyping, translocations, spatial transcriptomics.

> [!question]- **Q134.** T4ccp biosensor: reporter, and why in vivo homologous recombination.
> **A:** Reporter = cytochrome c peroxidase (CCP) from *S. cerevisiae*; infection and lysis of viable *E. coli* K-12 releases CCP → chromogenic colour. T4's ~169 kb genome with hydroxymethylcytosine cannot be digested/ligated directly *in vitro*, so the cassette `[g69]-[ccp]-[socmrh2]` on `pCRT4ccp` recombines by double crossover into the phage chromosome inside the host.

> [!question]- **Q135.** Why plaque hybridization is needed for T4ccp, and the recombinant frequency.
> **A:** T4wt and T4ccp form identical clear lytic plaques, so no phenotypic selection exists. Recombinants are < 0.1% of progeny; a DIG-labelled *ccp* probe hybridises only to T4ccp plaques, anti-DIG-AP/HRP gives a dark spot, and the plaque is picked and purified 2–3 rounds.
