## Chapter 1: Macrobiomolecules & Cell Structure

### 1. Physiological significance of ER localization and morphology
*   **Significance:** The Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) is localized adjacent to the nucleus because it is structurally continuous with the outer nuclear membrane. This allows for the efficient transfer of mRNA from the nucleus directly to ribosomes on the **Rough ER (RER)** for immediate translation into proteins destined for the secretory pathway.
*   **Morphology vs. Function:** The ER's extensive folded membrane system (cisternae) provides a large surface area for biochemical reactions. 
    *   **Rough ER:** Studded with ribosomes for protein synthesis and initial folding (assisted by chaperones like **BiP** and enzymes like **PDI** for disulfide bond formation).
    *   **Smooth ER:** Lacks ribosomes; functions in lipid synthesis and detoxification.

### 2. Protein sorting and targeting
*   **Molecular Basis:** Proteins contain specific **signal sequences** (amino acid tags) that act as "zip codes."
*   **Mechanisms:**
    *   **Secretory Pathway:** Signal sequences guide proteins to the ER, then to the Golgi, and finally to lysosomes, the plasma membrane, or secretion.
    *   **Organelle Targeting:** Uses specific complexes like **TIM/TOM** for mitochondria or **nuclear pores** for the nucleus.
*   **Recognition:** Specificity is achieved through the interaction between signal sequences and their corresponding **receptors** or translocons on the target organelle membranes.

### 3. Extracellular signal response
*   **Structures:** The cell uses **Glycoproteins and Glycolipids** on the cell surface for recognition. **Lipid Rafts** (specialized membrane microdomains) act as hubs for signaling molecules.
*   **Transduction:** Signals are received by receptors, often triggering intracellular cascades like the **TOR pathway (mTOR)**, which responds to nutrients and growth factors to regulate protein synthesis and metabolism.

### 4. Exons/Introns vs. Coding/Non-coding
*   **Exons/Introns (Structural):** Exons are segments of DNA that remain in the mature mRNA after **splicing**, while introns are intervening sequences that are removed.
*   **Coding/Non-coding (Functional):** Coding sequences (CDS) are the parts of exons that actually translate into protein. Non-coding sequences include introns, as well as the 5' and 3' Untranslated Regions (UTRs) of exons and regulatory RNAs (miRNA, siRNA).

### 5. Transposable Elements (TEs)
*   **Biological Roles:** TEs promote genomic diversity and the evolution of new regulatory networks (Advantageous). 
*   **Impacts:** 
    *   **Positive:** Source of genetic innovation and adaptation.
    *   **Negative:** Can cause lethal insertions, genomic instability, and diseases like cancer (Deleterious).

### 6. Macromolecular Interactions
*   **Nature:** These are primarily non-covalent, allowing for dynamic biological processes.
*   **Types:** 
    1. **Hydrogen bonds** (e.g., DNA base pairing).
    2. **Peptide bonds** (covalent; protein backbone).
    3. **Disulfide (S-S) bridges** (covalent; stabilizing protein structure).
    4. **Ionic bonds** (electrostatic).
    5. **Van Der Waals forces** (weak attractions).

### 7. Gel Electrophoresis Analysis
*   **Size Estimation:** Smaller DNA molecules migrate faster toward the positive electrode. Based on the ladder:
    *   **Sample A:** A single high-intensity band at ~500 bp.
    *   **Sample B:** Three bands at ~700 bp, ~500 bp, and ~200 bp.
    *   **Sample C:** Two bands at ~500 bp and ~200 bp.
*   **Intensity:** Fluorescence intensity is proportional to the **mass/concentration** of DNA in that band.
*   **Justification:** Differences in size occur due to different DNA fragment lengths (e.g., from restriction digestion). Intensity differences reflect the relative abundance of those specific fragments in the sample.

## Chapter 2: DNA Replication & Repair

### 1. DNA Stability and Flexibility
*   **Flexibility:** Essential for processes like **denaturation** (unwinding for replication), **mutation** (evolution), and **recombination**.
*   **Stability:** Crucial for preserving genetic information, maintained by the **double helix structure**, **renaturation**, and **repair mechanisms** (e.g., BER).
*   **Significance:** This balance allows life to be both resilient to damage (stability) and capable of change/growth (flexibility).

### 2. DNA Replication Events
*   **Events:** Unwinding (Helicase) $\rightarrow$ Priming (Primase) $\rightarrow$ Elongation (DNA Pol) $\rightarrow$ Ligation (Ligase).
*   **Replication Fork:** Mandatory because DNA Polymerase requires a single-stranded template and moves only in the 5' to 3' direction. Localized unwinding prevents the massive energy cost and instability of separating the entire genome at once.

### 3. Recognition, Proofreading, and BER
*   **Proofreading:** DNA Polymerase uses **3'-5' exonuclease activity** to remove mismatched nucleotides immediately after incorporation.
*   **BER (Base Excision Repair):** 
    1. **DNA Glycosylase** removes the damaged base.
    2. **AP Endonuclease** cuts the backbone.
    3. **DNA Pol $\beta$** fills the gap, and **Ligase** seals it.

### 4. Why RNA Primers?
*   DNA Polymerases cannot initiate synthesis *de novo*; they require a free **3'-OH group**. RNA Polymerases (Primase) can start without a primer. Evolutionarily, using an RNA primer allows the cell to "mark" the start of a strand for later removal, ensuring high fidelity since the very beginning of a fragment is often prone to errors.

### 5. End-Replication Problem
*   **Mechanism:** Because the lagging strand requires RNA primers, the very end of a linear chromosome cannot be fully replicated once the final primer is removed (there's no upstream 3'-OH).
*   **Aging:** This leads to the progressive shortening of **telomeres**, eventually triggering cellular senescence (aging).

### 6. Helicase Termination
*   In prokaryotes, helicase stops when it encounters specific **Termination (Ter) sites** bound by Tus proteins. In eukaryotes, termination occurs when two moving replication forks collide or when the end of the linear chromosome is reached.

### 7. Why Localized Unwinding?
*   Full separation would expose large amounts of single-stranded DNA to damage, require massive amounts of energy (ATP) to overcome the hydrogen bonds of the entire molecule, and cause extreme torsional stress (supercoiling).

### 8. DSB Repair
*   **Mechanisms:** **Homologous Recombination (HR)** (uses a template, error-free) and **Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ)** (ligates ends directly).
*   **Highest Risk:** **NHEJ** carries the highest risk of sequence loss because it often "cleans" the broken ends by removing nucleotides before ligation.

### 9. Simultaneous DSBs
*   If breaks occur at two loci simultaneously, the cell may incorrectly ligate the wrong ends together, leading to **chromosomal translocations**, large-scale deletions, and massive genomic instability.

### 10. Transposable Elements Impacts & Repression
*   **Impacts:** Evolutionary (diversity), Regulatory (new promoters), and Pathological (mutations).
*   **Repression:** Mammalian cells use **DNA methylation**, **KRAB/KAP1** histone modifications, **APOBEC** sequence editing, and **piRNA/PIWI** proteins to silence TEs.

### 11. SSB vs. DSB
*   Single-strand lesions (SSB) are less structurally dangerous because the intact complementary strand keeps the chromosome together. However, if not repaired, they can lead to base deamination or mismatches, which become permanent **point mutations** after subsequent rounds of replication.

## Chapter 3: Transcription & Regulation

### 1. Gene Components
*   **Promoter:** Site for RNA Pol binding (Initiation).
*   **RNA Coding Sequence:** Template for the RNA molecule.
*   **Terminator:** Signal to stop transcription.

### 2. Prokaryote vs. Eukaryote Transcription
*   **Prokaryote:** Occurs in cytoplasm; simultaneous with translation; uses a single RNA Pol and **Sigma ($\sigma$) factors**.
*   **Eukaryote:** Occurs in the nucleus; involves extensive processing (Capping, Splicing, Poly-A); uses three distinct RNA Polymerases (Pol I, II, III).

### 3. Initiation Complexity
*   Prokaryotes use a simple Holoenzyme (Pol + Sigma). Eukaryotes require a large **Pre-initiation complex** involving numerous General Transcription Factors (TFIID, etc.) and mediators. This reflects the need for more precise and complex regulation in multicellular organisms.

### 4. Promoter Strength
*   Determined by how closely the promoter sequence matches the **consensus sequence** (e.g., -10 and -35 boxes). Closer matches increase the **binding affinity** of RNA Polymerase, leading to more frequent transcription initiation and higher efficiency.

### 5. RNA Pol Interaction
*   The Sigma factor (prokaryotes) or TBP (eukaryotes) recognizes the promoter. RNA Pol then binds and undergoes a conformational change to melt the DNA strands, forming the **transcription bubble**.

### 6. 3' to 5' Sliding
*   RNA Pol must read the template in the 3' to 5' direction so that it can synthesize the new RNA strand in the **5' to 3' direction**. This reflects the biochemical property that nucleotides can only be added to the **3'-OH group** of the growing chain.

### 7. Spatial Paradox (Enhancers vs. Operators)
*   Bacterial **Operators** must be adjacent to the promoter to physically block RNA Pol (direct interference).
*   Eukaryotic **Enhancers** can work from a distance because the DNA between them loops out (**3D folding**), bringing the distal enhancer into physical contact with the promoter-bound complex via **Mediator** proteins.

### 8. Lac Operon Table Explanation
*   **Glu+, Lac-:** No transcription. High Glucose = No CAP activation; No Lactose = Repressor stays bound.
*   **Glu+, Lac+:** Low-level. Lactose removes Repressor, but high Glucose means no CAP to boost RNA Pol binding.
*   **Glu-, Lac-:** No transcription. CAP is bound, but the Repressor blocks the path.
*   **Glu-, Lac+:** Strong transcription. CAP boosts binding, and Lactose removes the Repressor "brake."

### 9. Lac vs. Trp Operons
*   **Lac (Inducible):** Normally "Off." Turned "On" by the presence of a substrate (Lactose).
*   **Trp (Repressible):** Normally "On." Turned "Off" by the presence of the end-product (Tryptophan).

## PhD Hoang My Dung Specifics

1.  **Proofreading:** Handled by the 3'-5' exonuclease domain of DNA Polymerase to ensure high fidelity.
2.  **TE Mechanism:** Class 1 (Retrotransposons) use an RNA intermediate and **Reverse Transcriptase**. Class 2 (DNA Transposons) use a "Cut and Paste" mechanism via **Transposase**.
3.  **Prokaryotic Termination:** 
    *   **Rho-independent:** Forms a G-C rich stem-loop followed by a U-rich tail that destabilizes the complex.
    *   **Rho-dependent:** Rho protein binds the RNA and "chases" the polymerase to unwind the DNA-RNA hybrid.
4.  **Transcription Bubble:** A localized region (~12-14 bp) where DNA is unwound. It maintains a DNA-RNA hybrid to allow the correct base pairing for the growing RNA strand.
5.  **Lac Operon Regulatory Mechanism:** Includes the **Promoter** (binding), **Operator** (repressor site), **lacI gene** (encodes repressor), and **CAP site** (glucose sensing).
6.  **Roles of Regulatory Components in Lac Operon:**
    *   **Repressor (LacI):** Binds to the operator to prevent transcription in the absence of lactose.
    *   **Inducer (Allolactose):** Binds to the repressor to cause its dissociation from the operator.
    *   **CAP (Catabolite Activator Protein):** Binds to the CAP site when cAMP levels are high (low glucose) to enhance RNA Pol binding and maximize transcription.
