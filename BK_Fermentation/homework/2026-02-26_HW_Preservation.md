# Fungal Preservation Protocols Report

Effective preservation of fungal cultures is vital for maintaining genetic integrity in fermentation technology. This report evaluates the Castellani method and cryopreservation as optimized strategies for stabilizing marine and endophytic isolates.

## 1. Short-Term Preservation: Sterile Distilled Water (Castellani Method)

The Castellani method utilizes nutrient deprivation to induce artificial dormancy, effectively bypassing the need for frequent subculturing. In their study on endophytic microorganisms, Costa et al. (2016) describe the protocol: 

> "Glass vials (20 ml) were filled with 10 ml of distilled water (autoclaved for two consecutive days). Five small fragments (25 mm2) taken from a 7 day culture and transferred to these vials. The vials were closed with a rubber stopper and an aluminium seal and stored at room temperature (28°C)."

The authors concluded that "conservation under distilled water was the most appropriate form of preservation" for genera like *Aspergillus* and *Penicillium*. This approach is particularly advantageous for extremotolerant marine isolates because it prevents pleomorphism and genetic drift common in nutrient-rich media. By suspending fungal blocks in sterile water, the basal metabolic rate is lowered without the contamination risks associated with mineral oil, ensuring immediate genetic stability for working stocks.

## 2. Long-Term Preservation: Cryopreservation in Liquid Nitrogen

For decades-long archiving, cryopreservation in liquid nitrogen (-165°C to -196°C) remains the gold standard. Amses et al. (2024) utilize a specialized matrix in their protocol:

> "The MEA consists of 2.7% malt extract (m/m) and 2.7% agar (m/m) mixed in warm tap water and supplemented with 5% glycerol (v/v) as a cryoprotectant (Homolka 2013). Approximately 1.5 mL of glycerol-supplemented MEA was transferred to each of the perlite-filled cryogenic vials, keeping ~1/4 of the total volume remaining as open headspace to allow for inoculation"

This method achieved exceptional results, where "502 (97.7%) sets resulted in 100% of tested inoculum passing viability standards." The 5% glycerol acts as a critical cryoprotectant, facilitating a vitrified state that prevents lethal ice crystal formation within the hyphae. Furthermore, the perlite substrate provides a high-surface-area matrix for mycelial adhesion, which is essential for the successful recovery of sensitive marine isolates like *Acremonium* or *Aspergillus* sourced from hydrothermal vents.
