Protease-Accelerated Fish Sauce Production
The attached paper argues that the best protease for fish sauce is not the enzyme with the highest laboratory
hydrolysis rate, but the one that still works under 20–25% salt, limits bitterness, preserves aroma development, and
stays within tight cost constraints (). Across the broader literature, that framing fits the evidence: proteases clearly
shorten fish sauce processing, but the practical winner is usually a staged system that combines initial proteolysis
with subsequent microbial maturation rather than a single fast endopeptidase alone (Lê et al., 2015; Yang et al.,
2025).


Mechanism

Endopeptidases such as Alcalase, trypsin, and papain cleave internal peptide bonds in actomyosin and collagen,
which rapidly lowers viscosity and drives liquefaction (Rianingsih et al., 2016). Exopeptidases then trim terminal
residues and are needed to raise amino nitrogen yield and release glutamate- and aspartate-rich amino acids that
build umami ().

A fast endopeptidase-only process tends to stall at bitter oligopeptides, especially hydrophobic 500–3000 Da
fragments, which is why the attached paper recommends endo/exo synergy instead of maximal cleavage speed
alone (). That pattern appears outside fish sauce too: alkaline protease alone reached 29.1% hydrolysis in clam
substrate, but adding an A. oryzae aminopeptidase complex increased hydrolysis to 49.8% while reducing
bitterness and improving umami (Zhao et al., 2026).

Cysteine proteases such as bromelain and the Bacillus megaterium KLP-98 acid protease act through a sulfhydryl-
dependent mechanism, whereas trypsin is a serine protease source commonly exploited from viscera or added
directly (Pramita et al., 2020; Fu et al., 2008; Ciou et al., 2020). The attached paper’s broader mechanistic point is
that catalytic class matters less than whether the enzyme’s pH and temperature window matches fish mash and
whether activity survives brine ().

Commercial enzymes still function in strong brine, but performance falls as salt rises. Protamex and Protex 51FP
remained active even at 30% NaCl over six hours, yet hydrolysis decreased progressively with higher salinity (Lê et
al., 2015). A salt-tolerant acid protease from B. megaterium retained only 5% activity at 25% NaCl, but still improved
anchovy sauce when temperature and pH were favorable (Fu et al., 2008).

The literature supports two workable process philosophies rather than one. One uses conventional high salt with
halotolerant enzymes or starters and accepts slower but authentic maturation (Nakano et al., 1986; Chau et al.,
2021; Bai et al., 2025). The other deliberately lowers salt or separates the steps, using one-week or shorter
hydrolysis at 7–8% salt and 21–45°C, then adding constraints such as pCO2, heating, or later salting to recover
safety and flavor control (Gao et al., 2020; Okubo et al., 2022).

Costs And Capacity

The attached paper gives the clearest economic rule: enzyme cost should stay within 2–5% of total raw material
cost per batch, and food-grade regulatory status is non-negotiable (). It also suggests realistic bulk enzyme pricing
below about $15–25/kg for crude fungal or bacterial preparations rather than purified recombinant enzymes ().

Direct large-scale fish sauce capacity data are sparse in the supplied papers, but the industry scale is large enough
that enzyme economics matter: Thailand alone is reported to produce more than 400 million liters annually
(Narayan & Gopal, 2023). For activity-based enzyme selection, Alcalase gave the lowest cost per unit activity and
the best protein recovery economics at 4.3 kg extracted protein per USD, while Flavourzyme gave higher
hydrolysis and lower bitterness but at higher cost (Valdivia et al., 2025).

Microbial enzyme production itself can be intensified, which matters if a manufacturer wants in-house protease
supply rather than purchased formulations. One optimized B. velezensis process reached 1160.3 U/mL protease
activity, but that study was still flask-scale and explicitly lacked techno-economic validation for industrial reactors
above 1000 L (Fergany et al., 2026).


Efficiency Equations

Protease efficiency in fish sauce is usually tracked by degree of hydrolysis or amino nitrogen release. One fish
sauce paper defines DH as 10% TCA-soluble nitrogen divided by total nitrogen ×100 (Narayan & Gopal, 2023):

[ DH(%)=\frac{N_{\text{TCA-soluble}}}{N_{\text{total}}}\times100 ]

The attached paper emphasizes A_N/T_N as the industrially meaningful target because free amino acids, not just
solubilized peptides, drive sauce quality (). A practical quality expression is therefore:

[ \text{Amino Nitrogen Ratio}(%)=\frac{A_N}{T_N}\times100 ]

Classical enzyme kinetics still matter for screening. The attached paper points to low (K_m) and high (k_{cat}/K_m)
on dense fish substrates as the theoretical ideal (). A characterized fish-sauce-relevant protease from B.
megaterium showed (K_m=2.1) mg/mL and (k_{cat}=6.1\ \text{s}^{-1}) against azocasein (Fu et al., 2008), and
another microbial protease study reported (K_m=0.207) mM with Michaelis-Menten behavior (Fergany et al.,
2026):

[ v=\frac{V_{\max}[S]}{K_m+[S]}, \qquad k_{cat}=\frac{V_{\max}}{[E]T}, \qquad \text{Catalytic
efficiency}=\frac{k{cat}}{K_m} ]

For comparing commercial proteases at equivalent activity, the most useful supplied model fits the hydrolysis
progress curve with a logarithmic equation and estimates specific activity from the slope of parameter (a) versus
dose (Valdivia et al., 2025):

[ P(t)=\frac{1}{b}\ln(1+abt) ]

where (P(t)) is product release or hydrolysis progress, (a) is the initial hydrolysis constant linked to enzyme dose,
and (b) captures the slowdown from substrate depletion or inactivation (Valdivia et al., 2025). The fitted progression
curves show why equivalent-dose comparison is better than comparing supplier label doses directly. (Fig 4:

Protease can clearly reduce fish sauce fermentation time, but the attached paper is right that the best industrial
choice is the enzyme system that balances salt tolerance, debittering, aroma maturation, safety, and cost, not
the one with the highest laboratory DH alone. A large-scale process is therefore best designed as controlled
liquefaction plus maturation, with efficiency judged by AAN/TN, DH, flavor, biogenic amines, and cost per useful
activity.
